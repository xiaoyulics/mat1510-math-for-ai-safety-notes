\section{Bayesian learning: the mixture}\label{sec:L5-bayes}
\tsm{11:50}
The instructor wants to discuss generalization \say{a little bit more}; the lecture continues Lecture~3 and stays within the second topic of the course outline, the theory of deep learning. He has been debating whether to talk more about Bayesian learning. He decides to do so, pitching it at \say{this imaginary person}: those who took statistics courses will find it boring, those who did not will feel rushed, and perhaps there is one person for whom it is exactly right. The point is to fix the basics and the notation, and then to see which parts are confusing and which are important. Training a neural network is not exactly Bayesian inference, but it is closely connected to it and is often approximated by it.

\paragraph{The setup.}\tsm{14:03}
We are given a sample $D_n=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i$ in some sample space $\mathcal X$; a class $\{p_\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}$ of probability distributions on $\mathcal X$, indexed by a parameter space $\Theta$ (in the main case a subset of $\R^d$, though not necessarily); and a \term{prior} $\pi$, a probability distribution on $\Theta$ that says which of the distributions we consider a priori better or worse. For simplicity the data points are assumed i.i.d.\ from some true distribution $q$. (A student asks whether they are i.i.d.\ or only conditionally i.i.d.\ given $\theta$; the answer is that they are simply i.i.d.\ from~$q$, which is all that we see.)

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{defn}[Bayes mixture, posterior, predictive]\label{def:L5-bayes}
The \emph{Bayesian probability} of the data, or \emph{evidence}, is the prior-weighted average of the probabilities that the individual parameters give to them,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L5-mixture}
  \Pbayes(D_n)\;=\;Z_n\;:=\;\int_\Theta\pi(\theta)\prod_{i=1}^np_\theta(x_i)\,\dd\theta .
\end{equation}
The \emph{posterior} reweights the parameters by how well they predicted the data, and the \emph{predictive} distribution of the next point averages their predictions with the posterior weights:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L5-posterior}
  \pi_n(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi(\theta)\prod_{i=1}^np_\theta(x_i)}{Z_n},\qquad
  \Pbayes(x_{n+1}\mid D_n)\;=\;\int_\Theta p_\theta(x_{n+1})\,\pi_n(\theta)\,\dd\theta .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}
This is the setting of Section~\ref{sec:L3-evidence} (with integrals in place of sums), and \eqref{eq:L5-posterior} is the continuous version of \eqref{eq:posterior}--\eqref{eq:predict}. The instructor makes three remarks.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item $\Pbayes$ \emph{is a probability distribution on $\mathcal X^n$}: one integrates over $x_n$ first and uses Fubini's theorem to exchange the order of integration; each $p_\theta$ integrates to one, and so does the prior.
  \item From the learning point of view this looks \say{maybe a stupid thing to do}: it asks how well the model of the world \emph{as a whole} predicts the data one has seen, whereas in learning one first looks at some data and then asks how well one predicts the next one. That is the predictive distribution $\Pbayes(x_{n+1}\mid D_n)$ of \eqref{eq:L5-posterior}.
  \item The normalizing constant of the posterior is \emph{exactly} $\Pbayes(D_n)$: one and the same number, \say{the same players coming in, in slightly different roles}. (The instructor first writes the wrong thing, is corrected by students, and then fixes the formula.) It follows that the predictive distribution is the ratio of two evidences,
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:L5-ratio}
     \Pbayes(x_{n+1}\mid D_n)\;=\;\frac{\Pbayes(D_{n+1})}{\Pbayes(D_n)}\;=\;\frac{Z_{n+1}}{Z_n},
  \end{equation}
  where $D_{n+1}$ is $D_n$ with $x_{n+1}$ appended.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{beyond}[Why these three statements hold, and what ``i.i.d.'' means here]
(a) By Tonelli, $\int\Pbayes(D_n)\,\dd x_n=\int\pi(\theta)\prod_{i<n}p_\theta(x_i)\big(\int p_\theta(x_n)\,\dd x_n\big)\dd\theta=\Pbayes(D_{n-1})$, and induction gives total mass~$1$; the same computation shows that the laws $\Pbayes$ on $\mathcal X^n$, $n\ge1$, are consistent, i.e.\ they are the laws of the first $n$ coordinates of a single random sequence. This is what makes ``$\Pbayes(D_{n+1})/\Pbayes(D_n)$'' a conditional probability of that sequence. (c) $\int p_\theta(x_{n+1})\pi_n(\theta)\,\dd\theta=\frac1{Z_n}\int\pi(\theta)\prod_{i\le n+1}p_\theta(x_i)\,\dd\theta=Z_{n+1}/Z_n$; telescoping with $Z_0=1$ gives the chain rule
\[
   \Pbayes(D_n)\;=\;\prod_{t=1}^{n}\Pbayes(x_t\mid D_{t-1}),\qquad -\log Z_n\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^n\log\frac1{\Pbayes(x_t\mid D_{t-1})},
\]
the evidence is the \emph{cumulative log-loss} of the sequential Bayesian predictor, as in Lecture~3. Finally, two different laws are in play: the data come from $q$ and are i.i.d.\ under~$q$, while under the mixture $\Pbayes$ the data points are \emph{exchangeable} but not independent (they are conditionally i.i.d.\ given~$\theta$, and each observation tells us something about~$\theta$ and so about the next one). Regret is an expectation under~$q$ of a quantity defined through $\Pbayes$.
\end{beyond}

\section{Code length, regret, and a recap of Lecture 3}\label{sec:L5-code}
\tsm{24:45}
The instructor then recalls the \say{Shannon--Kraft interpretation} of the quantity $\log\frac1{p(x)}$: it is the length of an optimal code for the event $x$, adapted to the probability $p$.

\paragraph{Code length.}\tsm{24:45}
Suppose there are countably many events. We want to describe frequent events by very short strings and rare ones by longer strings; the function $\log\frac1p$ is monotone decreasing in $p$ and equals zero at $p=1$. Kraft's inequality says that this can be done for any distribution over a countable collection of events, and Shannon's theorem says that in expectation one cannot do better, whatever other code one finds (Theorem~\ref{thm:kraft}). A student asks what ``adapted'' means: first one is given the probability; then, using it, one assigns to each event a string of zeros and ones, short for frequent events and longer for rare ones, so that the length is exactly $\log\frac1{p(x)}$ (up to the rounding to an integer).

\paragraph{Regret.}\tsm{27:27}
To compare a prediction with the optimal one, the reasonable thing to do is to ask how much longer the code is. The \term{regret} of the Bayesian predictor is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L5-regret}
  R_n\;=\;\KL\big(q^{(n)}\,\|\,\Pbayes^{(n)}\big)\;=\;\E_q\Big[\log\frac1{\Pbayes(D_n)}-\log\frac1{q(D_n)}\Big]\;=\;\E_q\Big[\log\frac{q(D_n)}{\Pbayes(D_n)}\Big],
\end{equation}
exactly as in \eqref{eq:regret}. It is non-negative: the expected code length is smallest for the code adapted to the true distribution~$q$ itself, so any other code is longer on average.

\paragraph{A derivative in $n$.}\tsm{30:05}
Look at the one-step quantity $\log\frac1{\Pbayes(x_{n+1}\mid D_n)}$. By \eqref{eq:L5-ratio} it equals $\log\frac1{\Pbayes(D_{n+1})}-\log\frac1{\Pbayes(D_n)}$, a difference of two code lengths in which $n$ changes by one, which for large~$n$ (\say{one is small compared to the scale}) one can think of as the \emph{derivative} of $n\mapsto\log\frac1{\Pbayes(D_n)}$. So even though the cumulative quantity is not what one cares about in learning (one cares about predicting the next point after learning from the previous ones), the prediction error is just its derivative. (The exact version is the chain rule recalled in Lecture~3: $R_{n+1}-R_n=\E\,\KL\big(q\,\|\,\Pbayes(\cdot\mid D_n)\big)$.)

\paragraph{Two classes of Bayesian predictors.}\tsm{31:42}
Last time two classes were considered (Sections~\ref{sec:L3-compression} and~\ref{sec:L3-singular}):
\begin{description}
  \item[Class 1: Solomonoff induction.] The hypotheses $h$ range over a countable collection of \emph{computable} probability distributions $p_h$, those that can be generated by a finite program. The mixture is $\Pbayes(D_n)=\sum_h2^{-\Kc(h)}p_h(D_n)$, with the \term{Solomonoff prior} $2^{-\Kc(h)}$, which penalizes programs of large length.
  \item[Class 2: a parametric family.] $\{p_\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}$ with $\Theta\subset\R^d$ and a prior $\pi$.
\end{description}
Two regret bounds were \emph{stated} last time (not derived): for Solomonoff's class $R_n\le\Kc(h^*)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:solomonoff}); for the parametric class, a result from the 1980s, Rissanen's $R_n\approx\frac d2\log n$ plus lower-order terms, under regularity assumptions (Theorem~\ref{thm:rissanen}); and if some of the regularity is relaxed, Watanabe's $R_n\approx\lambda\log n$ plus smaller terms, where the number $\lambda\le\frac d2$ depends on how singular the Kullback--Leibler divergence from the true distribution is on the parameter space (Theorem~\ref{thm:watanabe}).

\needspace{5\baselineskip}
\subsection{The Solomonoff bound at the board}\label{sec:L5-solomonoff-proof}\tsm{34:28}
The instructor proves the first bound. Assume the important hypothesis that the truth $q=p_{h^*}$ is itself one of the computable distributions. Then every term of the mixture is non-negative and one can keep only the term of the true hypothesis:
\[
  \Pbayes(D_n)\;=\;\sum_h2^{-\Kc(h)}p_h(D_n)\;\ge\;2^{-\Kc(h^*)}p_{h^*}(D_n)\;=\;2^{-\Kc(h^*)}\,q(D_n).
\]
Since $\log$ is monotone, \emph{for every} sample $D_n$
\[
  \log\frac{q(D_n)}{\Pbayes(D_n)}\;\le\;\log\frac{q(D_n)}{2^{-\Kc(h^*)}q(D_n)}\;=\;\Kc(h^*)\ln2 ,
\]
(with $\Kc$ counted in bits and $\log=\ln$), and in particular the expectation \eqref{eq:L5-regret} satisfies $R_n\le\Kc(h^*)\ln2$ nats, that is $\Kc(h^*)$ bits. The expectation was never used: \say{we have non-trivial mass on exactly the right hypothesis, and that is all we need}; all one needs is a bound on the point mass that the prior puts on the correct hypothesis. (This is the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:solomonoff}; the lecture's version works with the weights $2^{-\Kc(h)}$ directly, which sum to at most one by Kraft's inequality, so that $\Pbayes$ is a semimeasure; normalizing it, as in Lecture~\ref{ch:L3}, only increases $\Pbayes$ and the bound persists.) The bound is surprising next to the continuous case, where the regret grows like $\frac d2\log n$.

\begin{discussion}[What is $R_n$, and can the bounds be attained?]
A student asks whether the regret is a proxy for time or for loss. The instructor: for \emph{loss}. Part of the loss is reducible and part is not: there is a loss that comes from the randomness of $q$ itself, which no learner can avoid; $R_n$ is what one pays in addition for picking a learning method that is not optimal. Is the bound an inequality or can it be an equality? It can be an equality: there are distributions for which Solomonoff induction attains it, and similarly in the parametric case there are truths for which the Bayesian predictor has exactly that regret; one can be \say{very lucky} and learn them quickly, but in general this is what one sees.
\end{discussion}

\begin{beyond}[Two cases in which the bounds are attained, and the loss per sample]
\emph{Solomonoff.} Take a finite class of \emph{deterministic} sequences with the uniform prior. After $n$ observations the mixture gives the observed prefix the probability $\pi(h^*)\cdot\#\{\text{hypotheses that agree with }h^*\text{ on the first }n\text{ symbols}\}$, so $R_n=\ln\frac{|\mathcal H|}{\#\{\text{agreeing}\}}$, which equals $\ln\frac1{\pi(h^*)}$ as soon as the other hypotheses are ruled out: the bound $\log_2|\mathcal H|$ of Figure~\ref{fig:L3-regret} is reached. \emph{Parametric.} In the Gaussian example of Section~\ref{sec:L5-gauss} the regret equals $\frac d2\ln(n+1)$ plus an explicit bounded remainder, so the $\frac d2\log n$ of Rissanen's theorem is not an artifact of an upper bound. \emph{Loss per sample.} The ``derivative in $n$'' of that regret, the excess loss of the Bayesian predictor on the next point, is
\[
  R_{n+1}-R_n\;=\;\frac d2\ln\Big(1+\frac1{n+1}\Big)+\frac{|\theta^*|^2-d}{2(n+1)(n+2)}\;\approx\;\frac d{2n},
\]
so the reducible part of the loss decays like $1/n$ with a coefficient equal to half the number of parameters: a power law with exponent $1$, the parametric rate, for a regular model (and $\lambda_{\mathrm{RLCT}}/n$ for a singular one, Section~\ref{sec:L3-watanabe}); the data exponents of Lecture~\ref{ch:L2} are a different matter, they depend on the spectrum.
\end{beyond}
